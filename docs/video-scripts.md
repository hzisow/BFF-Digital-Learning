# Video scripts

Scripts for the nine BFF Academy lessons that do not have a recording yet, four
core and five electives. Written to match the four already recorded: about three
minutes each, three or four checkpoint beats, and a title in the form
"Watch: <the question the video answers>".

Beats are marked `[CHECKPOINT]` rather than timed. Timings have to come off a
real transcript, because a mark here says where a beat sits in the writing, not
where it lands in a recording.

The recording and wiring steps are in STATUS.md, under "Adding the rest of the
videos later".

## Earning Income

**Lesson:** Core lesson 1  ·  `earning-income`

**Title:** Watch: Where Your Money Actually Comes From

Most people think there is one way to make money. You work, you get paid, done.
There are actually two whole categories, and knowing the difference changes how
you think about every job you will ever take.

Earned income is money you work for. You show up, you do something, you get
paid. Unearned income is money your money makes while you are asleep. Interest
in a savings account, dividends from a stock, rent from a property you own.
Almost everyone starts with only earned income. The goal over a lifetime is to
build some of the other kind.

`[CHECKPOINT: earned vs unearned]`

Earned income shows up in three flavors. Wages pay you by the hour, so 20 hours
at $15 an hour is $300. Salary pays you a fixed amount for the year no matter
how many hours it takes, which is great some weeks and brutal in others.
Commission pays you a cut of what you sell, so a car salesperson making 3
percent on a $30,000 car earns $900 on that one sale.

`[CHECKPOINT: the three flavors]`

So what actually moves your number? Four things. Education, because more
training usually opens higher-paying doors. Seniority, because the person who
has been there six years knows things the new hire does not. Skills and
experience, which is the one you have the most control over right now.
And the economy itself, which nobody controls. When an industry is booming it
pays more, and when it is not, it does not.

`[CHECKPOINT: what raises your pay]`

Here is the part that surprises everyone on their first payday. The number on
your offer letter is not the number that hits your bank account. That first
number is gross income, before anything comes out. What lands in your account is
net income, after taxes, after Social Security and Medicare, after anything you
signed up for like health insurance. The gap is often 20 to 30 percent.

So when someone offers you $20 an hour, do the math on what you will actually
take home before you plan how to spend it. Read your paystub every single time.
That is where your money went, itemized, and it is the only place you will catch
a mistake before it costs you.

`[CHECKPOINT: gross vs net]`

## Financial Decision-Making

**Lesson:** Core lesson 6  ·  `financial-decision-making`

**Title:** Watch: Every Choice Has a Price

Somebody hands you $50 right now. You can buy the shoes you have been watching,
split it across three smaller things you also want, or put it straight into
savings. There is no right answer. But every one of those choices costs you the
other two, and that is the whole idea behind smart money decisions.

Economists call it opportunity cost. It is the value of the thing you gave up.
Buying the $50 shoes does not cost you $50, it costs you the concert ticket you
did not buy. That reframe is small and it changes everything, because now you
are comparing two real things instead of comparing one thing to a number.

`[CHECKPOINT: opportunity cost]`

There are three tools that make this easier. The first is comparison shopping,
which is not just checking two prices. It is checking price, quality, warranty,
and what the thing actually costs to keep owning. A $40 pair of headphones that
dies in four months is more expensive than an $80 pair that lasts three years.

The second is a cost-benefit analysis. Write the costs on one side and the
benefits on the other. Not in your head. On paper, or in your notes app. Half
the time the decision makes itself the moment you see it written down.

`[CHECKPOINT: comparison shopping and cost-benefit]`

The third is delayed gratification, and it is the hardest one. Waiting 24 hours
before any purchase over some amount you pick, maybe $30, kills a shocking
number of buys you would have regretted. The urge is real and it is also
temporary. Companies design checkout flows specifically to get you before it
passes.

`[CHECKPOINT: delayed gratification]`

Now use the same tools on financial services, because a bank is a product too.
Say EasyBank has no minimum balance and no monthly fee, but only 12 ATMs in your
whole city and an app that crashes. SecurePlus has a $5 monthly fee, ATMs
everywhere, and a better app. Which is better? Depends entirely on you. If you
withdraw cash twice a week, $60 a year buys you a lot of convenience. If you
never touch cash, you just found $60.

That is the pattern. Name what you are giving up, compare real options against
each other rather than against nothing, and give yourself a beat before you
commit. Do that consistently and you will not make perfect decisions. You will
make defensible ones, which is the actual goal.

## Financial Planning

**Lesson:** Core lesson 7  ·  `financial-planning`

**Title:** Watch: Turning "Someday" Into a Number and a Date

Fast-forward fifteen years. Where are you living, what are you driving, what are
you doing on a Tuesday? Most people have a picture. Almost nobody has a plan,
and the difference between those two is entirely about specifics.

"I want to save money" is not a goal. It is a mood. A goal is specific,
measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound. Watch what happens when you
fix that sentence: "I want to save $600 for a laptop by June 1st by putting away
$50 a month." Now you know if you are on track or behind, and you know it every
single month instead of finding out at the end.

`[CHECKPOINT: what makes a goal SMART]`

Once you have a few goals, you have to rank them, because your money cannot go
everywhere at once. The order that works for almost everyone is the same.
Emergencies first, then debt that is actively costing you, then everything else.

An emergency fund comes first because without one, the first flat tire turns
into credit card debt and undoes months of progress. Start with $500. That
single number handles most of what actually goes wrong in a teenager's life.

`[CHECKPOINT: what comes first]`

Then attack high-interest debt, because a credit card charging you 24 percent is
a hole you cannot save your way out of. Paying that down is a guaranteed 24
percent return, which is better than almost anything you can invest in.

After that you can spread out. Save for the car, the trip, the deposit on a
first apartment. Long-term retirement money starts here too, and starting at 22
instead of 32 is worth hundreds of thousands of dollars later.

`[CHECKPOINT: high-interest debt]`

The last piece is milestones. Certain things are coming whether you plan for
them or not. A first car, moving out, tuition, insurance in your own name.
Writing down roughly when each one arrives turns them from emergencies into
line items. A $2,000 used car in three years is $56 a month. Nobody panics about
$56 a month. Everybody panics about $2,000 in a week.

Plans change constantly, and that is fine. The point is not predicting your life
correctly at seventeen. The point is that when something changes, you are
adjusting a plan instead of starting from nothing.

## Consumer Protection

**Lesson:** Core lesson 8  ·  `consumer-protection`

**Title:** Watch: How to Spot a Scam Before It Costs You

An email lands in your inbox. First National Bank, official logo, and it says
your account has been locked. Click here to restore access. Your heart rate goes
up a little, and that reaction is the entire point of the message.

That is phishing. Someone pretending to be an organization you trust, to get
something out of you. Usually your password, sometimes your money, often both.
And it works on smart people constantly, because it is not testing intelligence.
It is testing whether you are in a hurry.

`[CHECKPOINT: what phishing is]`

Every scam leaks the same tells. Urgency, because a real bank does not give you
four hours. Threats, because fear stops you from checking. An email address that
is almost right, like security at first-national-bank dot co instead of dot com.
Requests for information a real company already has. And any payment in gift
cards, which no legitimate business on earth will ever ask for.

If two or more of those show up at once, stop. Do not click, do not reply. Open
a new tab, type the company's real address yourself, and log in the normal way.
If something is genuinely wrong, it will be waiting for you there.

`[CHECKPOINT: the red flags]`

You are not on your own here. The Federal Trade Commission takes scam reports
and goes after the companies behind them. The Consumer Financial Protection
Bureau handles banks, lenders, and debt collectors. The Better Business Bureau
tracks complaints so you can look a business up before you hand over money.
Reporting takes about five minutes and it is how patterns get caught.

`[CHECKPOINT: who protects you]`

The worst version of this is identity theft, where somebody uses your name and
Social Security number to open accounts you never asked for. You often find out
months later, when a card you never applied for shows up on your credit report.

Three habits prevent most of it. Use a different password everywhere, because
one leak should not unlock your whole life. Turn on two-factor authentication so
a stolen password alone is useless. And check your credit report once a year for
free, which is the fastest way to catch an account with your name on it that you
did not open.

Being careful is not the same as being paranoid. Slow down, verify separately,
and never let someone else's urgency become yours.

## Your First Paycheck

**Lesson:** Elective  ·  `first-paycheck`

**Title:** Watch: Where Did My Paycheck Go?

You worked 40 hours at $15 an hour. That is $600. You already spent it in your
head. Then the deposit lands and it says $478, and your first honest reaction is
that somebody made a mistake.

Nobody made a mistake. $600 is your gross pay, which is what you earned. $478 is
your net pay, which is what you keep. Everything between those two numbers is
sitting on your paystub, itemized, and most people never read it.

`[CHECKPOINT: gross vs net]`

Start with income tax withholding. Instead of hitting you with one enormous bill
every April, the government takes a slice out of every check as you go. You are
paying the same tax either way. This just spreads it out so it does not wreck
you all at once.

Next is FICA, and this one confuses everybody because it is not really a tax on
you so much as a subscription you are enrolled in. It is 7.65 percent of your
pay, split between Social Security and Medicare. Your employer quietly pays the
same amount again on your behalf. That money funds retirement and health
coverage for people who are retired right now, and one day it funds yours.

`[CHECKPOINT: what FICA is]`

Then there is the form that controls all of it, the W-4. You fill it out on day
one and then forget it exists. It tells your employer how much to withhold. Get
it wrong in one direction and you owe money in April. Get it wrong the other way
and you handed the government an interest-free loan all year. Neither is a
disaster, but it is worth a look after any big life change.

`[CHECKPOINT: the W-4]`

Here is the habit almost nobody has. Read every stub. Check the hours against
what you actually worked, check the pay rate, check that a raise you were
promised actually showed up. Payroll errors are common and they are almost never
malicious, but they also do not fix themselves. The person most likely to catch
one is you, and only if you look.

At the end of the year all of this gets summarized on a W-2, which is what you
use to file your taxes. Keep it. And if you had tax withheld but earned very
little, filing is often how you get that money back.

Your paystub is not paperwork. It is a receipt for your own work.

## Taxes Deep-Dive

**Lesson:** Elective  ·  `taxes-deep-dive`

**Title:** Watch: How Tax Brackets Really Work

Almost everything you have heard about tax brackets from a relative at a family
dinner is wrong. The most common version goes like this: "Careful, if you make
one more dollar you jump a bracket and take home less." That is not how it
works, and believing it has talked real people out of real raises.

Start with what taxes actually buy. Roads, public schools, firefighters, food
safety inspections, the military, and the bridges you drive over without
thinking about it. You can argue about the amount. You cannot really opt out of
the list.

`[CHECKPOINT: what taxes fund]`

The federal income tax is progressive, which means higher income is taxed at
higher rates. But here is the part everyone misses. The higher rate applies
only to the dollars inside that bracket, not to all of your income.

Use round numbers. Say the first $11,000 you earn is taxed at 10 percent, and
everything from $11,000 to $45,000 is taxed at 12 percent. If you earn $45,000,
you do not pay 12 percent on all of it. You pay 10 percent on the first $11,000,
which is $1,100, and 12 percent on the remaining $34,000, which is $4,080.

`[CHECKPOINT: how brackets stack]`

Which kills the myth. A raise that pushes you into a higher bracket only taxes
the new dollars at the higher rate. Every dollar below the line keeps its old
rate. Taking a raise always leaves you with more money. Always. If someone tells
you otherwise, they are describing a system that does not exist.

`[CHECKPOINT: the raise myth]`

Two more terms worth knowing. Your marginal tax rate is the rate on your next
dollar, which is the bracket you are in. Your effective rate is what you actually
paid across all your income, and it is always lower. In the example above you
paid $5,180 on $45,000, so your effective rate is about 11.5 percent even though
your marginal rate is 12.

Finally, the standard deduction. Before any of this math runs, a chunk of your
income is simply not taxed at all. For most students that means a summer job may
owe nothing in federal income tax. If your employer withheld some anyway, filing
a return is how you get it back.

File anyway, even when you do not have to. That is often free money sitting with
the IRS waiting for you to ask.

## Paying for College

**Lesson:** Elective  ·  `paying-for-college`

**Title:** Watch: The Sticker Price Is Not the Price

You look up a college and the website says $58,000 a year. You close the tab.
That reaction is understandable and it is also a mistake, because almost nobody
pays that number.

That is the sticker price. The number that matters is the net price, which is
sticker minus everything you do not have to pay back. Two students at the same
school can pay wildly different amounts, and the expensive-looking private school
is sometimes cheaper than the state school once aid lands.

`[CHECKPOINT: sticker vs net price]`

Everything starts with one form, the FAFSA. It is free, it opens in the fall of
your senior year, and it is the single door to federal grants, work-study, and
federal loans. Many states and colleges also use it to decide their own aid.
Skipping it because you assume you will not qualify is the most expensive
assumption in this entire lesson. Fill it out. Every year.

`[CHECKPOINT: what FAFSA unlocks]`

What comes back is a menu, and the items are not equal. There is a golden order.

Grants first, because that is money you do not repay, usually based on financial
need. Scholarships next, also free, usually based on something about you.
Work-study third, because that is a job on campus with hours built around your
class schedule. Then subsidized loans, where the government covers the interest
while you are in school. Unsubsidized loans last, because those start charging
interest the day the money arrives.

Take everything at the top of that list before you touch anything at the bottom.

`[CHECKPOINT: the golden order]`

Now compare offers properly. Two award letters can look similar and be nothing
alike. School A: $58,000 sticker, $30,000 in grants, $8,000 in loans, so you owe
$20,000 out of pocket and graduate $8,000 in debt. School B: $28,000 sticker,
$3,000 in grants, $12,000 in loans, so you owe $13,000 up front and graduate
$12,000 in debt. Cheaper today, more expensive later.

Do that math on paper for every offer. Also do not sleep on community college.
Two years there and two at a state school can cut the total cost roughly in
half, and your diploma says the school you graduated from.

Treat this like an investment, because it is one. What does the degree cost, and
what does it realistically pay?

## Entrepreneurship & Side Hustles

**Lesson:** Elective  ·  `entrepreneurship`

**Title:** Watch: Start Embarrassingly Small

You do not need a degree, an office, or anybody's permission to start a business.
Teenagers run real ones every day. Mowing lawns, tutoring algebra, designing
logos, fixing phone screens. Entrepreneurship sounds enormous, and underneath it
is just solving a problem for somebody and getting paid for it.

So start with the problem, not the idea. The best businesses come from noticing
something annoying that people already deal with. Parents who cannot find a
babysitter on short notice. Classmates who need a decent headshot. Neighbors with
a yard nobody wants to touch. If you cannot name who is annoyed and what they are
currently doing instead, you do not have a business yet.

`[CHECKPOINT: finding the problem]`

Then there is one formula you cannot skip. Revenue minus costs equals profit.

Revenue is everything customers pay you. Costs are everything you spend to
deliver it. Profit is what you actually keep, and it is the only number that
matters. If you charge $25 to mow a lawn and spend $6 on gas, you made $19, not
$25. Get that wrong and you can be busy every weekend and broke every month.

`[CHECKPOINT: revenue, costs, profit]`

Pricing is where most people undercharge themselves into quitting. Too low and
you are working for free and attracting customers who complain. Too high with no
track record and nobody bites. Find the range by looking at what others charge,
then price slightly under while you build up reviews, and raise it once you have
proof you are good.

`[CHECKPOINT: pricing]`

Two things to know before you scale. Reinvestment: when the money starts coming
in, putting some back into better equipment or advertising is how a side hustle
becomes an actual business instead of a hobby that pays. And taxes, because
self-employed income is not withheld for you. If a client pays you more than
$600 in a year you may get a Form 1099, and either way that income is reportable.
Set aside roughly a quarter of what you make and you will not get ambushed.

Last thing, and it is the most useful. Start embarrassingly small. Your first
version should be almost too simple. One service, one price, five customers you
already know. You learn more from five real customers in a month than from six
months of planning a business you never launched.

## Crypto & Modern Money Traps

**Lesson:** Elective  ·  `crypto-and-scams`

**Title:** Watch: "Get Rich Quick" Is Always the Tell

Your feed is full of it. Coins going to the moon, somebody your age in front of
a rented Lamborghini, a betting app promising an easy parlay, a checkout button
whispering four easy payments. Some of that is real technology. Some of it is
gambling in a costume. Today is about telling them apart.

Cryptocurrency is digital money that is not issued by any government or bank.
Instead of a bank keeping the records, transactions live on a blockchain, a
shared public ledger copied across thousands of computers, which makes the record
very hard to fake. The technology is genuinely clever. Here is the catch. Most
crypto has no cash flow behind it. No rent, no profits, no interest. The price is
purely what the next person will pay.

`[CHECKPOINT: what crypto actually is]`

Which brings up volatility, meaning how wildly a price swings. A savings account
moves like a slow escalator. Insured, boring, always up. Crypto moves like an
untested rollercoaster. Bitcoin has dropped more than 50 percent in a few months
on multiple occasions, and plenty of smaller coins went to zero and stayed there.

So money you actually need, your emergency fund, car savings, college money, does
not belong in something that can lose half its value before homecoming. Saving
and speculating are different sports with different rules.

`[CHECKPOINT: volatility vs saving]`

Now the traps. A pump-and-dump is when insiders hype something to drive the price
up, then sell their own holdings to the people who believed them, and the price
collapses. If a stranger online is urgent about getting you in early, you are not
early. You are the exit.

Betting apps and loot boxes work on a different principle called the house edge.
The game is built so that over enough plays the house wins. Not usually, not
mostly. Mathematically. You can win tonight. You cannot win forever.

`[CHECKPOINT: pump-and-dump and the house edge]`

The last one is aimed straight at your brain. FOMO, the fear of missing out, is
engineered into countdown timers and "only 3 left" banners and everyone else
posting their wins and never their losses. Buy now, pay later is the same trick
at checkout. Splitting a $60 hoodie into four payments makes it feel free, and
missed-payment fees can turn it into $95.

One rule covers almost all of this. If it promises fast, guaranteed, or
risk-free returns, it is selling you something. Real investing is slow and
boring, and slow and boring is what actually works.
